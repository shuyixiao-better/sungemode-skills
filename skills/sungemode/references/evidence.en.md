# Evidence and decision rules

## Label claims that influence a decision

| Type | Meaning | Record |
|---|---|---|
| Fact | Observed or verifiable information | E ID, source/link, raw observation, date, scope |
| Inference | A conclusion derived from evidence | E IDs, reasoning, counterexamples |
| Assumption | A proposition awaiting a test | H ID, test, rejection condition |
| Unknown | Missing or inaccessible information | Gap, verification route, whether it blocks a decision |

A public pricing page supports the listed price, not actual sales, market size, or your expected income. Positive comments are attitude feedback, not observed usage, payment, or a verifiable commitment.

## Verification

- Define the claim before choosing sources. For pricing, record currency, billing period, region, tier, and access date. For demand, record recruitment and how participants differ from target customers.
- Prefer official product/pricing pages, documentation, primary statistics, and original customer feedback. A promotional page and a repost are not independent evidence.
- Preserve the scope and timing of conflicting sources rather than averaging them into false certainty. Flag inaccessible pages; search snippets do not prove you read the full page.
- Match sources to claims, place citations beside claims, and distinguish publication, observation, and access dates.
- Without browsing, design a research route but never invent links, competitor facts, prices, interview participants, or completed research.

## Decisions

Check hard constraints first: budget, time, reachable customers, and delivery capability. Then compare demand, acquisition cost, delivery cost, personal fit, and evidence quality. High scores cannot compensate for missing evidence; preferences rank choices but do not establish demand.

Set a primary metric and thresholds before the experiment. Keep denominators and recruitment bias visible. Record time and cash separately. Thresholds are negotiable decision rules, but never quietly move the goalposts after results: preserve the original value, reason, and new test.

Separate failure to execute, failed acquisition, lack of customer pain, and delivery failure. Small samples or absent controls warrant an insufficient-evidence conclusion, not market validation.
